善い少年の物語

昔、ジェイコブ・ブリヴェンズという善い少年がいた。両親の要求がどれほど馬鹿げ、理不尽でも、いつも従った。いつも教科書を勉強し、日曜学校に遅れることはなかった。落ち着いて判断すれば学校をさぼるのが最も有益だと分かるときでさえ、さぼらなかった。ほかの少年たちには、この子がまるで理解できなかった。あまりに奇妙な振る舞いをしたからだ。どれほど都合がよくても嘘をつかなかった。嘘は悪いことだと言い、それだけで彼には十分だった。正直すぎて、まったく馬鹿げて見えた。ジェイコブの変わったやり方ときたら、何もかも度を越していた。
Once there was a good little boy by the name of Jacob Blivens. He always obeyed his parents, no matter how absurd and unreasonable their demands were; and he always learned his book, and never was late at Sabbath school. He would not play hookey, even when his sober judgment told him it was the most profitable thing he could do. None of the other boys could ever make that boy out, he acted so strangely. He wouldn't lie, no matter how convenient it was. He just said it was wrong to lie, and that was sufficient for him. And he was so honest that he was simply ridiculous. The curious ways that that Jacob had, surpassed everything.
日曜日にビー玉遊びをせず、鳥の巣を荒らさず、オルガン弾きの猿に熱した硬貨を渡さなかった。理にかなった娯楽には、何一つ興味がないようだった。そこでほかの少年たちは、理由を考え、彼を理解しようとしたが、満足な結論には至れなかった。先ほど言ったように、ただ漠然と、あいつは「病気」なのだろうという考えに行き着いただけだった。そのため彼を保護下に置き、誰にも危害を加えさせなかった。
He wouldn't play marbles on Sunday, he wouldn't rob birds' nests, he wouldn't give hot pennies to organ-grinders' monkeys; he didn't seem to take any interest in any kind of rational amusement. So the other boys used to try to reason it out and come to an understanding of him, but they couldn't arrive at any satisfactory conclusion. As I said before, they could only figure out a sort of vague idea that he was "afflicted," and so they took him under their protection, and never allowed any harm to come to him.
この善い少年は日曜学校の本を一冊残らず読んだ。それが何よりの楽しみだった。秘密はそこにあった。彼は日曜学校の本に出てくる善い少年たちを信じきっていた。彼らに絶対の信頼を置いていた。一度でいいから、生きている善い少年に出会いたかったが、ついに出会えなかった。たぶん、みな彼が生まれる前に死んだのだろう。特に善い少年の話を読むと、すぐ結末をめくり、その子がどうなったか確かめた。何千マイル旅をしてでも会って見つめたかったからだ。だが、いつも無駄だった。善い少年は最終章で必ず死に、葬式の絵が載っていた。親戚と日曜学校の子どもたちが墓の周りに立ち、短すぎるズボンや、大きすぎるボンネットを身につけ、みな一ヤード半もの布を使ったハンカチに顔を埋めて泣いていた。いつもこうして先を越された。あの善い少年たちは最終章で必ず死んでしまうので、一人にも会えなかった。
This good little boy read all the Sunday school books; they were his greatest delight. This was the whole secret of it. He believed in the good little boys they put in the Sunday school books; he had every confidence in them. He longed to come across one of them alive, once; but he never did. They all died before his time, maybe. Whenever he read about a particularly good one he turned over quickly to the end to see what became of him, because he wanted to travel thousands of miles and gaze on him; but it wasn't any use; that good little boy always died in the last chapter, and there was a picture of the funeral, with all his relations and the Sunday school children standing around the grave in pantaloons that were too short, and bonnets that were too large, and everybody crying into handkerchiefs that had as much as a yard and a half of stuff in them. He was always headed off in this way. He never could see one of those good little boys on account of his always dying in the last chapter.
ジェイコブには、日曜学校の本に載るという気高い野心があった。母に嘘をつくのを立派に拒み、母が喜びの涙を流す絵。戸口に立って六人の子を連れた貧しい物乞いの女に一ペニーを渡し、好きに使ってよいが、浪費は罪だからぜいたくはしないように、と教える絵。学校帰りに角で待ち伏せし、板切れで頭を打ち、「ひゅう、ひゅう！」とはやしながら家まで追いかける悪い少年のことを、寛大にも告げ口しない絵。そんな絵とともに載りたかった。それが若いジェイコブ・ブリヴェンズの野心だった。
Jacob had a noble ambition to be put in a Sunday school book. He wanted to be put in, with pictures representing him gloriously declining to lie to his mother, and her weeping for joy about it; and pictures representing him standing on the doorstep giving a penny to a poor beggar-woman with six children, and telling her to spend it freely, but not to be extravagant, because extravagance is a sin; and pictures of him magnanimously refusing to tell on the bad boy who always lay in wait for him around the corner as he came from school, and welted him over the head with a lath, and then chased him home, saying, "Hi! hi!" as he proceeded. That was the ambition of young Jacob Blivens.
彼は日曜学校の本に載りたかった。ただ、善い少年が必ず死ぬことを思うと、ときどき少し不安になった。生きるのが好きだったから、日曜学校の本の少年になるうえで、これは最も不愉快な点だった。善くあることは健康によくないと知っていた。本の少年たちのように超自然的なほど善くあることは、結核より命取りだとも、誰一人長く耐えられなかったとも知っていた。本に載せてもらえば自分がその本を見られないかもしれず、死ぬ前に出版されても、後ろのほうに葬式の絵がなければ人気が出ないだろうと思うと、つらかった。死に際に地域の人々へ授けた助言を書けない本など、立派な日曜学校の本とはいえない。そこで結局、事情の許すかぎり最善を尽くすことにした――正しく生き、できるだけ長く持ちこたえ、自分の時が来たらすぐ使えるよう、臨終の演説を用意しておくのだ。
He wished to be put in a Sunday school book. It made him feel a little uncomfortable sometimes when he reflected that the good little boys always died. He loved to live, you know, and this was the most unpleasant feature about being a Sunday school book boy. He knew it was not healthy to be good. He knew it was more fatal than consumption to be so supernaturally good as the boys in the books were; he knew that none of them had ever been able to stand it long, and it pained him to think that if they put him in a book he wouldn't ever see it, or even if they did get the book out before he died it wouldn't be popular without any picture of his funeral in the back part of it. It couldn't be much of a Sunday school book that couldn't tell about the advice he gave to the community when he was dying. So at last, of course, he had to make up his mind to do the best he could under the circumstances—to live right, and hang on as long as he could, and have his dying speech all ready when his time came.
ところが、どういうわけか、この善い少年には何一つうまくいかなかった。本の中の善い少年と同じ結末にならない。あちらはいつも楽しく過ごし、悪い少年の脚が折れる。ところがジェイコブの場合、どこかのねじが緩んでいて、すべて反対に起きた。ジム・ブレイクがりんごを盗んでいるのを見つけ、よそのりんごの木から落ちて腕を折った悪い少年の話を読んで聞かせようと木の下へ行くと、ジムはたしかに落ちたが、ジェイコブの上に落ちてジェイコブの腕を折り、ジムは無傷だった。ジェイコブには理解できなかった。本にはそんなことは書かれていなかった。
But somehow nothing ever went right with this good little boy; nothing ever turned out with him the way it turned out with the good little boys in the books. They always had a good time, and the bad boys had the broken legs; but in his case there was a screw loose somewhere, and it all happened just the other way. When he found Jim Blake stealing apples, and went under the tree to read to him about the bad little boy who fell out of a neighbour's apple-tree and broke his arm, Jim fell out of the tree too, but he fell on him, and broke his arm, and Jim wasn't hurt at all. Jacob couldn't understand that. There wasn't anything in the books like it.
またあるとき、悪い少年たちが盲人を泥の中へ突き飛ばした。ジェイコブが走り寄り、助け起こして祝福を受けようとすると、盲人は祝福などせず、杖で頭を殴り、もう一度自分を突き倒してから助けるふりをするところを捕まえたら、ただではおかないと言った。どの本とも話が合わなかった。ジェイコブは全部を調べた。
And once, when some bad boys pushed a blind man over in the mud, and Jacob ran to help him up and receive his blessing, the blind man did not give him any blessing at all, but whacked him over the head with his stick and said he would like to catch him shoving him again, and then pretending to help him up. This was not in accordance with any of the books. Jacob looked them all over to see.
ジェイコブがぜひしたかったことの一つは、住む場所もなく、飢え、いじめられている足の悪い犬を見つけ、家へ連れて帰ってかわいがり、その犬から永遠に消えない感謝を受けることだった。ついに一匹を見つけ、幸福になった。家へ連れ帰って餌をやり、かわいがろうとすると、犬は飛びかかり、前側にある衣服以外をすべて引き裂いて、彼を驚くべき見世物にした。典拠を調べても、理解できなかった。本に出てくる犬と同じ犬種なのに、まるで違う振る舞いをした。この少年は何をしても災難に遭った。本の少年たちが褒美をもらう行いは、彼が手を出せる中で、いちばん割に合わない投資になった。
One thing that Jacob wanted to do was to find a lame dog that hadn't any place to stay and was hungry and persecuted, and bring him home and pet him and have that dog's imperishable gratitude. And at last he found one and was happy; and he brought him home and fed him, but when he was going to pet him the dog flew at him and tore all the clothes off him except those that were in front, and made a spectacle of him that was astonishing. He examined authorities, but he could not understand the matter. It was of the same breed of dogs that was in the books, but it acted very differently. Whatever this boy did he got into trouble. The very things the boys in the books got rewarded for turned out to be about the most unprofitable things he could invest in.
あるとき日曜学校へ向かう途中、悪い少年たちが帆船で遊びに出ようとしているのを見た。本を読んでいたので、日曜日に舟遊びへ行く少年は必ず溺れると知っており、恐ろしくなった。いかだで漕ぎ出して警告しようとすると、丸太が足元で回転し、川へ滑り落ちた。すぐ男に引き上げられ、医者が水を汲み出し、ふいごで息を吹き返させたが、風邪をひいて九週間寝込んだ。それなのに、悪い少年たちは一日じゅう楽しく過ごし、驚いたことに全員無事で元気に帰宅した。ジェイコブ・ブリヴェンズは、こんなことは本には書かれていないと言った。まったく呆然としていた。
Once, when he was on his way to Sunday-school, he saw some bad boys starting off pleasuring in a sail-boat. He was filled with consternation, because he knew from his reading that boys who went sailing on Sunday invariably got drowned. So he ran out on a raft to warn them, but a log turned with him and slid him into the river. A man got him out pretty soon, and the doctor pumped the water out of him, and gave him a fresh start with his bellows, but he caught cold and lay sick a-bed nine weeks. But the most unaccountable thing about it was that the bad boys in the boat had a good time all day, and then reached home alive and well in the most surprising manner. Jacob Blivens said there was nothing like these things in the books. He was perfectly dumbfounded.
回復すると少し気落ちしたが、それでも挑戦を続けると決めた。これまでの経験では本に載せてもらえないと分かっていたが、善い少年に割り当てられた寿命にはまだ達していない。時が満ちるまで持ちこたえれば、まだ記録を作れると期待した。ほかがすべて失敗しても、臨終の演説が残っている。
When he got well he was a little discouraged, but he resolved to keep on trying anyhow. He knew that so far his experiences wouldn't do to go in a book, but he hadn't yet reached the allotted term of life for good little boys, and he hoped to be able to make a record yet if he could hold on till his time was fully up. If everything else failed he had his dying speech to fall back on.
典拠を調べると、今こそ給仕見習いとして船へ出るべき時だった。船長を訪ね、志願した。推薦状を求められると、誇らしげに小冊子を取り出し、「愛情深い先生より、ジェイコブ・ブリヴェンズへ」という文を指さした。だが船長は粗野で下品な男だった。「そんなもの、くそくらえだ！　皿洗いや残飯桶の扱いを知っている証拠にはならん。おまえはいらん」と言った。これはジェイコブの一生で、まったく前例のない出来事だった。小冊子に書かれた先生の賛辞は、必ず船長の最もやさしい感情を動かし、船長が与えられる名誉と利益のある役職すべてへの道を開いてきた――少なくとも彼が読んだ本では、失敗したためしがない。自分の感覚を信じられなかった。
He examined his authorities, and found that it was now time for him to go to sea as a cabin-boy. He called on a ship captain and made his application, and when the captain asked for his recommendations he proudly drew out a tract and pointed to the words, "To Jacob Blivens, from his affectionate teacher." But the captain was a coarse, vulgar man, and he said, "Oh, that be blowed! that wasn't any proof that he knew how to wash dishes or handle a slush-bucket, and he guessed he didn't want him." This was altogether the most extraordinary thing that ever happened to Jacob in all his life. A compliment from a teacher, on a tract, had never failed to move the tenderest emotions of ship captains, and open the way to all offices of honour and profit in their gift—it never had in any book that ever he had read. He could hardly believe his senses.
この少年はいつもひどい目に遭った。典拠どおりになることが何もなかった。ついにある日、諭すべき悪い少年を探して歩いていると、古い製鉄所で少年たちの一団を見つけた。彼らはちょっとした冗談の準備中で、十四、五匹の犬を長い列につなぎ、尾に空のニトログリセリン缶を取りつけて飾ろうとしていた。ジェイコブの心は動かされた。義務が前にあると油汚れなど気にしなかったので、缶の一つに腰を下ろし、先頭の犬の首輪をつかみ、悪いトム・ジョーンズへ咎める眼差しを向けた。ちょうどそのとき、怒りに満ちたマクウェルター市会議員が入ってきた。
The boy always had a hard time of it. Nothing ever came out according to the authorities with him. At last, one day, when he was around hunting up bad little boys to admonish, he found a lot of them in the old iron foundry fixing up a little joke on fourteen or fifteen dogs, which they had tied together in long procession, and were going to ornament with empty nitro-glycerine cans made fast to their tails. Jacob's heart was touched. He sat down on one of those cans (for he never minded grease when duty was before him), and he took hold of the foremost dog by the collar, and turned his reproving eye upon wicked Tom Jones. But just at that moment Alderman McWelter, full of wrath, stepped in.
悪い少年たちは全員逃げたが、ジェイコブ・ブリヴェンズは潔白の自覚を胸に立ち上がり、日曜学校の本に出てくる格調高い演説を始めた。善い少年も悪い少年も、実際には誰一人「おお、旦那様！」で話を始めないという事実に真っ向から逆らって、必ず「おお、旦那様！」で始まる演説だ。しかし市会議員は続きなど待たなかった。ジェイコブの耳をつかんで向きを変え、平手で尻をぴしゃりと打った。その瞬間、善い少年は屋根を突き抜けて飛び出し、太陽へ向かって舞い上がった。十五匹の犬の破片が、凧の尾のように後ろへ連なっていた。
All the bad boys ran away, but Jacob Blivens rose in conscious innocence and began one of those stately little Sunday-school-book speeches which always commence with "Oh, sir!" in dead opposition to the fact that no boy, good or bad, ever starts a remark with "Oh, sir." But the alderman never waited to hear the rest. He took Jacob Blivens by the ear and turned him around, and hit him a whack in the rear with the flat of his hand; and in an instant that good little boy shot out through the roof and soared away towards the sun, with the fragments of those fifteen dogs stringing after him like the tail of a kite.
市会議員も古い製鉄所も、地上から跡形なく消えた。若いジェイコブ・ブリヴェンズは、あれほど苦労して用意した臨終の演説を、とうとうする機会を得られなかった――鳥たちに向かってしたのでなければ。というのも、彼の大部分は隣の郡の木の梢へ無事落ちたものの、残りは四つの郡区へ分配されたので、死んだのか、どうしてそうなったのかを突き止めるため、五回も検死審問を開かなくてはならなかった。あれほど散らばった少年は、見たことがないだろう。
And there wasn't a sign of that alderman or that old iron foundry left on the face of the earth; and, as for young Jacob Blivens, he never got a chance to make his last dying speech after all his trouble fixing it up, unless he made it to the birds; because, although the bulk of him came down all right in a tree-top in an adjoining county, the rest of him was apportioned around among four townships, and so they had to hold five inquests on him to find out whether he was dead or not, and how it occurred. You never saw a boy scattered so.
こうして、できるかぎり善くあろうとしたのに、本どおりの結末にならなかった善い少年は死んだ。同じことをした少年は、彼を除けばみな栄えた。彼の事例は、まことに注目すべきものである。おそらく永遠に説明されないだろう。
Thus perished the good little boy who did the best he could, but didn't come out according to the books. Every boy who ever did as he did prospered except him. His case is truly remarkable. It will probably never be accounted for.
